<Section id="plain" title="In plain words">
<Analogy>
Doing simple maths in a quiet room is easy. Doing it while someone talks to you, the TV is on and your phone keeps buzzing is surprisingly hard.

The maths didn’t get harder. Your brain just had less room for it.
</Analogy>

Our brains have limited room for thinking at any moment. Everything on a screen uses some of it. When too much competes for that room, people slow down, make mistakes and feel tired.
</Section>

<Section id="why" title="Why it happens">
Educational psychologist John Sweller described two kinds of mental load that matter for design:

<ul class="examples">
  <li><strong>Intrinsic load</strong><p>The effort the task itself requires, like choosing a flight or understanding a contract. You can’t remove it, only support it.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Extraneous load</strong><p>Effort caused by the design, like clutter, confusing labels, inconsistent layouts and distractions. This is the part designers can and should remove.</p></li>
</ul>

When extraneous load is high, there’s less room left for the real task. That’s why a busy screen makes a simple job feel hard.

<div class="callout">
  <strong>Low load doesn’t mean empty.</strong> A screen can contain a lot of information and still feel easy, if it’s well organised, consistent and shows things in a predictable order.
</div>
</Section>

<Section id="examples" title="Real-world examples">
<ul class="examples">
  <li><strong>Apple Pay</strong><p>Paying needs one glance and a double-click, with no card numbers to type and nothing to decide.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Google Maps driving mode</strong><p>Everything except the route and the next turn fades away while you drive.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Medium and Substack</strong><p>Reading pages hide menus and sidebars so all your attention goes to the text.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Stripe’s dashboard</strong><p>Complex financial data is shown with a clear hierarchy: key numbers first, details on demand.</p></li>
</ul>
</Section>

<Section id="apply" title="How to apply it">
<Checklist>
- **Remove clutter**: decorative elements, duplicate information and distractions that don’t help the task.
- **Use plain, familiar words** instead of internal jargon.
- **Be consistent**, so people don’t have to relearn layouts and patterns on each screen.
- **Reveal complexity gradually.** Show the basics first and put details one step away.
- **Do the work for people** with defaults, calculations and remembered choices (see Tesler’s Law).
</Checklist>
</Section>
