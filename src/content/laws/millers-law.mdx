<Section id="plain" title="In plain words">
<Analogy>
Someone reads you a shopping list out loud with no paper to write it on. Milk, eggs, bread: easy. By the eleventh item, the first ones have quietly fallen out of your head.

Your short-term memory is a small table, not a warehouse.
</Analogy>

There’s a limit to how much we can juggle in our heads at once. When an interface asks people to remember more than that, they forget, make mistakes or give up.
</Section>

<Section id="why" title="Why it happens">
In 1956, psychologist George Miller noticed that in many experiments people could hold about **seven items, give or take two**, in short-term memory. Later research suggests the real number is often even lower, around **four chunks**, depending on what the items are.

The exact number matters less than the lesson: **memory is limited, and it’s easy to overload**.

<div class="callout">
  <strong>Common mistake:</strong> “Miller’s Law says menus must have at most 7 items.” Not quite. When the options are <em>visible</em>, people don’t need to remember them. The limit matters when people must <em>hold</em> information in their head, like a code, a step from earlier, or details from another screen.
</div>
</Section>

<Section id="examples" title="Real-world examples">
<ul class="examples">
  <li><strong>Phone numbers</strong><p>We write them as 555-123-4567 instead of 5551234567, so you remember three groups instead of ten digits.</p></li>
  <li><strong>One-time codes</strong><p>Most login codes are only 6 digits, and phones often offer to fill them in automatically, so you don’t have to remember them at all.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Checkout summaries</strong><p>Good checkouts keep your order summary visible on every step, instead of making you remember what was in your cart.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Amazon product comparison</strong><p>Comparison tables put specs side by side so you don’t have to memorize them while flipping between pages.</p></li>
</ul>
</Section>

<Section id="apply" title="How to apply it">
<Checklist>
- **Keep information visible** when people need it, instead of asking them to remember it from a previous screen.
- **Chunk long content**, such as numbers, codes, forms and long pages, into small, meaningful groups.
- **Keep codes short** and offer copy, paste and autofill.
- **Use recognition, not recall.** Let people pick from options instead of typing things from memory.
- **Don’t treat “7” as a hard rule** for visible lists. Treat it as a reminder that memory is limited.
</Checklist>
</Section>
