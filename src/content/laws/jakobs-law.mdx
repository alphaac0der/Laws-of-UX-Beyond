<Section id="plain" title="In plain words">
<Analogy>
You can drive almost any car without reading the manual. The steering wheel, pedals and indicators are always in the same places.

Now imagine a car where the brake is on the steering wheel. It might be a clever idea, but you’ll crash before you get used to it.
</Analogy>

People don’t learn your website from scratch. They bring habits from **every other site and app** they use. When yours works the way they expect, it feels easy without them having to think.
</Section>

<Section id="why" title="Why it happens">
Over time, people build **mental models**: expectations about how things work. The logo in the top-left goes home. The cart is in the top-right. Underlined blue text is a link. These expectations come from thousands of hours on other products.

When your design matches them, people can spend their attention on their actual goal. When it breaks them, they have to stop, look around and relearn, and many won’t bother.

<div class="callout">
  <strong>This doesn’t mean “never innovate.”</strong> It means be different where it matters and familiar everywhere else. If you do change a convention, make sure the benefit is worth the learning cost.
</div>
</Section>

<Section id="examples" title="Real-world examples">
<ul class="examples">
  <li><strong>Online shops</strong><p>Almost every store puts search at the top and the cart in the top-right corner, so shoppers never have to hunt.</p></li>
  <li><strong>YouTube’s redesigns</strong><p>When YouTube changes its layout, it often lets people try the new version early and switch back, giving them time to adjust.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Google Docs</strong><p>It copied the familiar toolbar and shortcuts of Microsoft Word, so new users felt at home right away.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Mobile apps</strong><p>Pull to refresh, swipe to delete and the bottom tab bar work the same across thousands of apps.</p></li>
</ul>
</Section>

<Section id="apply" title="How to apply it">
<Checklist>
- **Study the conventions** in your type of product before designing, such as how other shops, banks or editors do it.
- **Keep standard elements where people expect them:** logo, navigation, search, cart, account and settings.
- **Use familiar names** like “Cart”, “Sign in” and “Settings” instead of clever ones.
- **When you redesign, ease people in.** Offer a preview, a short explanation, or a temporary way back to the old version.
- **Innovate in your core value**, not in basic controls like buttons, forms and menus.
</Checklist>
</Section>
