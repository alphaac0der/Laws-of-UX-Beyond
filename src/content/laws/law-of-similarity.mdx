<Section id="plain" title="In plain words">
<Analogy>
At a football match, you instantly know who’s on which team. Players don’t stand in groups; they’re mixed all over the field.

The shirt colors do all the grouping for you.
</Analogy>

When things share a **color, shape, size or style**, we assume they’re related and work the same way. That’s a powerful shortcut, and it can also mislead people if similar-looking things behave differently.
</Section>

<Section id="why" title="Why it happens">
Our visual system groups similar things automatically, before we consciously think about it. It’s one of the ways we make sense of a busy scene quickly. Color is the strongest cue, followed by shape and size.

In interfaces, this means **consistency teaches people**. Once someone learns that blue underlined text is a link, they expect every blue underlined thing to be a link. Break that pattern, and they get confused.

<div class="callout">
  <strong>Common mistake:</strong> styling plain text the same as links, or making a heading look like a button. If it looks clickable, people will click it, and if it’s not, they’ll think the site is broken.
</div>
</Section>

<Section id="examples" title="Real-world examples">
<ul class="examples">
  <li><strong>Hyperlinks</strong><p>Across the web, colored and underlined text means “clickable.” Similarity across millions of sites taught us that.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Google Calendar</strong><p>Each calendar has its own color, so you can spot all your work events at a glance across the week.</p></li>
  <li><strong>GitHub labels</strong><p>Issues with the same colored label (like “bug” in red) are quickly recognized as one type.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Design systems</strong><p>Material, Apple and others make all primary buttons look the same, so users always know where the main action is.</p></li>
</ul>
</Section>

<Section id="apply" title="How to apply it">
<Checklist>
- **Give each kind of element one consistent style**: links, primary buttons, secondary buttons and tags.
- **Use color to group categories**, but always pair it with a label or icon for people who can’t see the difference.
- **Don’t style non-clickable things like clickable ones.**
- **Make the important item different** when you want it to stand out (see the Von Restorff Effect).
- **Document styles in a design system** so the whole team stays consistent.
</Checklist>
</Section>
