<Section id="plain" title="In plain words">
<Analogy>
Most people believe turning a thermostat up higher makes a room heat up faster. It doesn’t, the heater works at the same speed. But that belief still decides how people use it.

What people think is happening matters more than what’s actually happening.
</Analogy>

Everyone carries a simple “story” of how a product works. When the design matches that story, it feels obvious. When it doesn’t, people make mistakes and blame themselves, or your product.
</Section>

<Section id="why" title="Why it happens">
Psychologist Kenneth Craik suggested in 1943 that people build small-scale models of the world in their heads to predict what will happen. These models come from **past experience**, they’re often incomplete, and they don’t need to be technically correct to be useful.

There are always two models in play: the **designer’s model** (how it’s built) and the **user’s model** (how they think it works). Good design closes the gap between them, usually by adapting to the user, not the other way round.

<div class="callout">
  <strong>Common mistake:</strong> organising a product around its internal structure, like database tables, departments or code modules. Users don’t think in those terms, so they can’t find anything.
</div>
</Section>

<Section id="examples" title="Real-world examples">
<ul class="examples">
  <li><strong>The desktop and trash can</strong><p>Computers borrowed files, folders and a bin from real offices, so people could rely on what they already knew.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Shopping carts</strong><p>Online shops use “cart” and “checkout” because everyone understands how a real shop works.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Google Drive’s “Shared with me”</strong><p>It matches how people think about files (“someone gave me this”), not how they’re stored.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Natural scrolling</strong><p>Touchscreens made content move with your finger, like pushing paper, and laptops later followed to match that model.</p></li>
</ul>
</Section>

<Section id="apply" title="How to apply it">
<Checklist>
- **Research how users think**: interviews, card sorting and watching people use similar products.
- **Use their words** for labels and navigation, not internal or technical terms.
- **Build on familiar metaphors** like carts, folders, inboxes and switches.
- **Make the system’s state visible**, so people can check and correct their understanding.
- **Test early.** If people predict the wrong outcome, change the design, not the users.
</Checklist>
</Section>
