<Section id="plain" title="In plain words">
<Analogy>
Think of a long meeting. You probably remember how it started and the last thing that was said before everyone left.

The middle half hour? A bit of a blur.
</Analogy>

In any list, we remember **the beginning** and **the end** best. Items in the middle are the most likely to be forgotten.
</Section>

<Section id="why" title="Why it happens">
Two effects work together:

<ul class="examples">
  <li><strong>Primacy effect</strong><p>The first items get the most attention and mental rehearsal, so they move into long-term memory.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Recency effect</strong><p>The last items are still fresh in short-term memory when we need them.</p></li>
</ul>

The middle items get neither advantage. Hermann Ebbinghaus first described this pattern in the 1880s while testing his own memory with lists of made-up syllables.

<div class="callout">
  <strong>Try your results again.</strong> If your chart from the demo has tall bars at both ends and a dip in the middle, you just saw this effect in your own memory.
</div>
</Section>

<Section id="examples" title="Real-world examples">
<ul class="examples">
  <li><strong>Apple’s website navigation</strong><p>The Apple logo (home) is first, and search and the bag are last, with product categories in between.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Mobile tab bars</strong><p>Instagram puts Home first and Profile last, the two places people go most.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Landing pages</strong><p>Strong pages open with the main promise and close with a clear call to action, because those are what visitors remember.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Presentations</strong><p>Good speakers state the key point at the beginning and repeat it at the end.</p></li>
</ul>
</Section>

<Section id="apply" title="How to apply it">
<Checklist>
- **Put the most important items first and last** in menus, tab bars and lists.
- **Lead with the key message** on pages and emails, and end with a clear next step.
- **Keep lists short**, so fewer items fall into the forgettable middle.
- **Make middle items stand out** if they matter, for example with highlighting (see the Von Restorff Effect).
- **Summarize at the end** of long content, like checkouts, articles and onboarding.
</Checklist>
</Section>
