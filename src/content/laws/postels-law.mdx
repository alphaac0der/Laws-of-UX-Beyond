<Section id="plain" title="In plain words">
<Analogy>
A good waiter understands “a flat white”, “one flat white please” and “can I get a flat white?” as the same order.

A bad waiter says “Invalid order format. Please say: ONE FLAT WHITE.”
</Analogy>

People type, click and speak in messy, human ways. A good interface **understands what they meant**, instead of forcing them to match one exact format.
</Section>

<Section id="why" title="Why it happens">
Jon Postel wrote this rule for the early internet: computers talking to each other should be **liberal in what they accept and conservative in what they send**. That way, small differences don’t break the conversation.

The same goes for people. There are many ways to write a date, a phone number or a name. If your form only accepts one of them, people get errors for things that aren’t really mistakes, and every error costs time and trust.

<div class="callout">
  <strong>Flexible doesn’t mean sloppy.</strong> Accept many formats, but always store and show the result in one clear, consistent format, so people can check that you understood them correctly.
</div>
</Section>

<Section id="examples" title="Real-world examples">
<ul class="examples">
  <li><strong>Google Search</strong><p>It understands typos, missing words and casual questions, and still shows the right results (“Did you mean…?”).</p></li>

<Section id="apply" title="How to apply it">
<Checklist>
- **Accept common formats** for dates, phone numbers, card numbers and postcodes. Strip spaces and symbols yourself.
- **Be forgiving with case and whitespace**, for example in emails and codes.
- **Show back what you understood** in a clear, standard format.
- **Give helpful errors** that say exactly what’s needed, not just “invalid.”
- **Support different devices and inputs**, like touch, keyboard, voice, autofill and paste.
</Checklist>
</Section>
