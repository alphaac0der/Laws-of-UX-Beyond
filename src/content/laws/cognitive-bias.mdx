<Section id="plain" title="In plain words">
<Analogy>
A shop sells a jacket “was $200, now $120.” Next door, the same jacket is just “$120.” The first one feels like a bargain.

It’s the same jacket at the same price. Only the first number in your head changed.
</Analogy>

Our brains make thousands of decisions a day, so they take **shortcuts**. Most of the time these work well. But they also lead us to predictable mistakes, and those mistakes affect how people use every product.
</Section>

<Section id="why" title="Why it happens">
Psychologists Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman showed that people don’t weigh every option carefully. Instead, we rely on quick rules of thumb, called **heuristics**. These are fast and usually good enough, but they create consistent **biases**.

A few that show up constantly in design:

<ul class="examples">
  <li><strong>Framing</strong><p>The same fact feels different depending on how it’s worded (“90% fat-free” vs “10% fat”).</p></li>
  <li><strong>Anchoring</strong><p>The first number we see shapes how we judge the next ones, like an original price next to a sale price.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Default bias</strong><p>We tend to stick with whatever is pre-selected.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Confirmation bias</strong><p>We notice information that supports what we already believe, and that includes designers reading their own research.</p></li>
</ul>

<div class="callout">
  <strong>Use this power responsibly.</strong> Biases can help people (like a sensible default) or exploit them (like a pre-ticked “add insurance” box). Tricks that work against users are called dark patterns. They damage trust, and in many countries they’re illegal.
</div>
</Section>

<Section id="examples" title="Real-world examples">
<ul class="examples">
  <li><strong>Organ donation</strong><p>Countries where donation is the default (opt-out) have far higher donor rates than opt-in countries. Defaults are powerful.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Pricing pages</strong><p>A very expensive “Enterprise” plan makes the middle plan look reasonable. That’s anchoring at work.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Savings apps</strong><p>Apps that round up purchases and save the change use defaults and small amounts to help people save without effort.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Booking sites</strong><p>“Only 2 rooms left!” messages use scarcity bias. Helpful when true, manipulative when made up.</p></li>
</ul>
</Section>

<Section id="apply" title="How to apply it">
<Checklist>
- **Choose defaults that serve the user**, like the most common, safest or cheapest sensible option.
- **Word things clearly and honestly.** Test whether framing changes people’s understanding.
- **Show full prices** and real information. Never use fake scarcity or hidden costs.
- **Check your own bias** in research. Look for evidence that proves you *wrong*, not just right.
- **Ask: “Would users thank us if they knew?”** If not, it’s probably a dark pattern.
</Checklist>
</Section>
